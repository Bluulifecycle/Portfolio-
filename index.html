Case Study

Terra & Co. — Welcome Sequence

Note: Terra & Co. is a self-initiated concept project built to demonstrate welcome-sequence strategy and lifecycle automation skills. The brand, testimonials, and metrics referenced are illustrative, not results from a live client account.

Brand: Terra & Co., small-batch, natural skincare

Founder: Mary Whitfield

Hero product: Daily Glow Serum

Positioning: "Simple, natural, effective"

1. The Opt-In Experience

Minimal form fields built to lower conversion friction. The page backs each form with proof points: 100% natural ingredients, visible results in 2 weeks, and 50+ happy customers.

See the supporting page sections (product, proof points, reviews)

2. The 3-Part Automated Welcome Series

EMAIL 1 — Brand Discovery & Founder Welcome

Subject: Welcome to Terra & Co.
Preview: Here's why we started Terra&Co.

Matched the email templates to the opt-in form (same rounded corners, same button style, same product visual) so the experience feels continuous from click to inbox. Email 1 leads with the founder's story and the product itself before mentioning the discount, so the brand feels personal rather than promotional from the first message. The 10% code promised on the form then arrives in one highlighted block with a single CTA. In the flow, each subscriber is tagged as consent-pending (double opt-in) before the series starts.

EMAIL 2 — Hero Product Focus & Social Proof

Subject: The one bottle that replaced two.
Preview: Lisa's bathroom shelf used to look like a Sephora aisle.

Shifts from brand intro to hero-product positioning, introducing the Daily Glow Serum alongside customer reviews to address purchase hesitation, highlight routine simplification, and drive consideration through a dedicated CTA.

EMAIL 3 — Offer Introduction & Reassurance Close

Subject: Still thinking it over?
Preview: Your 10% off hasn't gone anywhere.

Written for subscribers who haven't bought yet: a gentle nudge, 4 days after signup, that the 10% code from Email 1 is still active. The sample build runs this email as part of the main series; the discount gating section below shows how I'd limit it to non-buyers in a live account.

3. Discount Gating

The 10% offer shouldn't go to everyone, and it shouldn't last forever. This is the conditional layer I'd add to the sequence in a live account. It uses the same tags and the Status: Customer segment already in the flow, and works the same way in Kit, Klaviyo, or Mailchimp. The automation map and segmentation setup are in sections 4–6 below.

4–6. Architecture, Segmentation & Results Targets

The deeper strategy behind the sequence. Tap to expand any section.

Technical Architecture & Backend Setup

Sequence mechanics

  • Trigger: form submission adds the subscriber to the welcome sequence.
  • Cadence: Email 1 immediately → Email 2 on day 2 → Email 3 on day 4.
  • Exit logic (designed): buyer status removes customers from non-buyer incentive reminders. See the discount gating section for the full rules.
  • Consent & deliverability: double opt-in verifies consent, with a target of keeping hard bounces under 0.5% to protect sender reputation.

Deliverability & compliance

Mobile-first, single-column templates with live HTML text and a balanced text-to-image ratio. Image-heavy emails often get routed to Gmail's Promotions tab, so this was a deliberate choice to stay in the primary inbox. CAN-SPAM/GDPR considerations included sender ID, physical address, unsubscribe function, and consent handling.

Segmentation & Lifecycle Strategy

Primary segments (strategy; the Status: Customer segment is built in the sample)

  • New Subscribers: entered through the 10% first-order offer
  • Engaged Non-Buyers: interacted with the sequence, haven't purchased
  • Customers: completed a purchase, exit acquisition messaging
  • Product-Interested Subscribers: shown interest via product-specific engagement
  • Re-Engagement Audience: declining engagement, needs a different approach

Lifecycle principle

Subscriber behavior determines what they receive next: Subscriber → Engaged Prospect → Customer → Retention. Once someone converts, they stop receiving first-purchase incentives. Messaging matches actual status, not signup date.

Proposed post-purchase flow

First Purchase → Product Education → Usage/Value Reinforcement → Cross-Sell → Repeat Purchase → Re-Engagement, driven by purchase data and engagement signals.

Testing & Optimization Framework

Subject lines, send timing, and offer framing would be tested and evaluated by conversion and revenue, not opens alone. Open rate is useful for diagnosing problems, not for reflecting real performance.

Variables to test

Subject-line positioning, offer framing, CTA language, send timing, product education angle, social-proof placement, segmentation criteria.

Primary evaluation metrics

First-order conversion rate, revenue per recipient, click-through rate, conversion from engaged non-buyers, unsubscribe rate, repeat-purchase rate.

Concept Work

Welcome email concepts

Two welcome emails I built on my own for brands I like. Each one starts with a brief and a goal and then walks through what every block is doing. I’m not claiming any results here. This is about the thinking.

UNWWORN: welcome email

Self-initiated concept · Fragrance for clothes · Discount welcome

Note: This is a self-initiated concept built from UNWWORN’s public brand assets. It isn’t affiliated with the brand and it isn’t a critique of anything they send. The code shown is an example.

Brief

A welcome email for a fragrance-for-clothes brand that promised new subscribers 10% off. The subscriber opened it to get the code.

Goal

Get the code into their hands straight away and make buying easy. Then introduce the founder so the next email has a voice behind it.

The thinking, block by block

1

The code

The code comes first in its own box on a photo of the bottle sitting in a shirt pocket. The photo shows what the product is for before any copy does. The deadline is set per subscriber through the ESP. Everyone gets five days from their own signup so a line like “ends Friday at midnight” is always true for the person reading it. The code is also generated and gated per subscriber. It works once and can’t be passed around.

2

The choice

Two sizes labelled Try first and Best value. The discounted price is already worked out with the original crossed out so nobody has to do the maths. A subscriber who is unsure can start small and one who is sold can go big. Making that decision easier for the customer tends to help conversion.

3

The founder

A polaroid and a short note from Meg with a promise that a second email will explain why she started the brand. It sits last because the subscriber came for the code. By the time they reach the note they already have what they came for and the promise gives the next email a reason to be opened.

4

The sign-off

The Instagram handle and a relaxed opt-out line: “Not an email person? Fair enough.” It keeps the brand’s tone going to the very end. An easy way out also tends to mean fewer spam complaints.

What I’d measure

  • How many people use the code within their five days
  • The split between the 30ml and 100ml buttons
  • Opens on the founder email two days later

SKIPD: waitlist welcome email

Self-initiated concept · Skincare · Waitlist welcome

Note: This is a self-initiated concept showing what a SKIPD waitlist welcome email could look like. It’s built from the brand’s public look and voice. It isn’t affiliated with SKIPD and the copy is mine.

Brief

A welcome email for a skincare waitlist. The subscriber has only given an email address and there is nothing to buy yet.

Goal

Confirm the signup and set the brand voice. Build some anticipation for launch and make it easy to reply.

The thinking, block by block

1

The confirmation

The email opens with “Done.” and asks nothing of the subscriber. That is the brand idea in action: skip the steps. The three tags underneath sum up the promise at a glance so someone who stops reading there still gets it.

2

The hook

One line about routines lasting about five days. Most people have been through that cycle so they nod along before the brand has said anything about itself. It also sets the product up as the answer to something they already know.

3

The teaser

A waitlist has no product to sell yet. The polaroid of the product in a back pocket gives the subscriber a glimpse without the full reveal. The “loading...” label turns the wait into something to look forward to and gives them a reason to open the next email.

4

The promise

Three numbered things set expectations early: early access, a little something at launch and a short email cadence. The last one matters most. People on a waitlist haven’t bought anything yet so knowing they won’t be flooded makes staying on the list an easier call.

5

The reply-driver

A soft button to Instagram and then a P.S. asking which skincare step they always skip. Anyone can answer that in one line so replying takes almost no effort. Replies are a good signal for a new sender and they give the brand its audience’s own words to use later.

What I’d measure

  • Reply rate with the P.S. question against a version without it
  • Clicks on the Instagram button against a plain text link
  • Opens on the next email to see if the teaser carries over

Audits

Unprompted teardowns

Three unsolicited lifecycle audits, sent directly to brands whose welcome flows had a clear gap between how good the rest of their brand looked and how generic their first email felt. Brand names are withheld, and findings are text only out of respect for the brands' own assets.

COMMUNITY-LED GEAR BRAND

Welcome Flow Optimization

The site has a sharp, intentional aesthetic, but the welcome emails default to system templates, including a broken merge tag that greets people by their raw email address.

Read full audit

Current state: friction and leaks

  • The opt-in step: the confirmation email forces a click-through before any brand story or welcome offer appears, causing drop-off at peak intent.
  • Broken personalization: the loyalty-score email has no fallback tag and greets users with their raw email address, which breaks the sense of a personal brand connection.
  • Trigger misalignment: the loyalty app fires a transactional points notification before the ESP has properly introduced the brand, so the sequence feels automated rather than curated.

The ideal state: a 3-part flow

Phase 1, Branded Welcome: replace the default confirmation email with a hero image matching the site's aesthetic, the founder's own voice pulled from the site copy, and a "Claim Your Welcome Offer" button in place of the default system message.

Phase 2, The Gear and The Vibe (24 hours): lead with the brand's 14,000+ customer community to build early trust, then tease the upcoming drop's 24-hour window to build a reason to keep opening.

Phase 3, The Loyalty Score Unveil (48 hours): fix the merge tags and reframe the loyalty points as a VIP club invite rather than an automated transaction.

The quick fix

Fixing the broken merge tags and replacing the generic confirmation wall with a branded HTML welcome flow should lift first-time buyer conversion and protect deliverability.

PREMIUM SLEEPWEAR BRAND

One Email Doing the Work of Three

A premium sleepwear brand sends a single welcome email with a flat discount, then goes quiet. On one of two test sends, Gmail also flagged that same email as unauthenticated.

Read full audit

What's already working

The wordmark and lifestyle photo set the right tone, and one of the two sends reached the inbox cleanly.

The sequence stops after email one

A thank-you note and a flat code, then nothing further. There is no story, no product education and no second or third touch, so anyone who doesn't buy off the first email is never followed up with.

The discount has no reason to act now

A flat 10% code with no expiration or countdown gives a new subscriber every reason to sit on it. Gating a stronger offer behind non-purchase, rather than handing away margin in email one, is the harder-working version.

The brand story isn't in the email yet

One lifestyle photo, a wordmark and a single line of copy. No fabric or sourcing story, no bestsellers and no social proof to earn a premium price point from someone who just met the brand.

The same email landed in spam

Two sends of the identical welcome email: one clean inbox delivery, one flagged by Gmail as unauthenticated. That points to an inconsistent SPF, DKIM and DMARC setup, and a welcome flow only works when every send reaches the inbox.

Recommended next step

  • Build a 3-part welcome sequence with dynamic discount gating.
  • Set up and verify SPF and DKIM for the exact domain the email sends from.
  • Publish a DMARC record and confirm From-domain alignment with DKIM.
  • Confirm the fix with a test send through mail-tester.com or Google Postmaster Tools.

EDITORIAL-VOICE BRAND

Welcome Flow Breakdown

The brand voice is the standout of the whole flow, but small inconsistencies, an inconsistent greeting, late social proof, an undated discount code, quietly leave conversion on the table.

Read full audit

What was received

Four emails over eight days after signup: a welcome email with the discount code, a cart reminder two days later, a founder story email, and a product email with customer quotes and press mentions on day eight.

What's already working

The voice carries the brand. Lines about pub gardens and packed commuter trains sound like a person, not a template, and the same editorial tone runs through Instagram and the packaging. The welcome email is short, warm, and delivers the code immediately.

Where it could tighten

  • One greeting everywhere: the cart reminder says "Hi Customer" while other emails say "Hi Friend". Capturing a first name at signup and using one fallback keeps the voice consistent.
  • Social proof sooner: customer quotes and press mentions don't arrive until day eight, after most undecided subscribers have already moved on. A lighter version could sit in the second or third email.
  • Give the code a shape: The code appears three times with no expiry or next step. A soft window and one low-pressure reminder gives people a reason to use it now.
  • Dark mode: the black wordmark nearly disappears against a dark background. A light logo variant keeps the brand visible for those readers.

One direction for the welcome email

Open with the product photography, use the packaging stripe as a border or divider, and keep the existing copy untouched. The code sits in its own boxed line above a single shop button, with a light logo for dark mode. This is a written concept, not a finished design.
